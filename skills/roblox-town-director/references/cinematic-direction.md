# Cinematic Direction and Visual Continuity Guide

Load during **Stages E–G** (`SHOOT`, `REVIEW`, `FULL`, and visual planning in `SCRIPT`).

## 1. Visual Language & Camera Motivation (Stage E)

Every camera placement must serve tension, spatial geography, point of view, or emotional distance—never output a mechanical list of angles without dramatic intent:
- **Shot Scale:**
  - *Wide / Establishing:* Grounds spatial geography and isolates a character against a street, house, or empty room.
  - *Medium / Two-Shot:* Reads posture, wardrobe contrast, and interpersonal distance.
  - *Tight / Over-the-Shoulder:* Draws attention to a doorway, prop, or hesitation beat. For standard live-gameplay avatars lacking facial micro-expressions, use over-the-shoulder POV, head turns, and spatial distance instead of extreme face close-ups. (Custom-animated 2D/3D faces require **Custom Roblox Animation** or **Hybrid Production**.)
- **Power Angles:** *Low angles* (camera low, tilted up) make doors, houses, or rivals feel imposing; *high angles* (camera high, tilted down) make a character look trapped, small, or watched.
- **Movement vs. Stillness:** A moving avatar inside a locked-off static frame—or an abrupt freeze after brisk walking—reads cleaner in Roblox than jittery camera panning. Always provide a **stationary camera fallback**.
- **Depth Staging:** Frame through doorways, stair railings, windows, or past foreground furniture to give flat game geometry depth.

## 2. Lighting & Atmospheric Contrast

- Avoid pitch-black rooms that turn into blocky compression artifacts on lower graphics settings or social video feeds.
- Build mood using **contrast and spill light**—a lit hallway spilling into a dim room, warm streetlights against a cool night street, or doorway silhouettes.

## 3. 4-Part Continuity System (Stage G)

1. **180-Degree Screen Direction:** Keep left-to-right spatial geography consistent across reverse-angle cuts so viewers never lose track of where characters stand relative to doorways or each other.
2. **Character & Wardrobe Lock:** Lock outfits, accessories, and held items before Take 1.
3. **Location, Lighting & Floor Marks:** Lock in-game time-of-day before shooting a scene. Use fixed landmarks (rug corners, doorframes, tile lines, curb edges) as repeatable actor marks across takes.
4. **Editing Handles (Trim Buffers):** Record enough additional footage to allow clean editing. Recommend 2–3 seconds of handles where practical, but adapt to the shot and recording method.
